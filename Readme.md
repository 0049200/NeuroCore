## 1. It Started with a Sound

NeuroCore did not begin as an attempt to build an artificial brain.

It began with a simple experiment in signal–system interaction.

A sound, represented as a data vector, was transmitted to an abstract circuit designated **(r)**, originally conceived as a simplified representation of the brain.

The circuit considered two conditions:

- **Signal present:** The circuit receives an external input.
- **Signal absent:** The circuit operates without that input.

The difference between these conditions provided information about how the circuit's internal state changed in response to the signal.

The original idea was not merely to recognize sound.

**The signal became a reference through which the system could observe its own response and potentially regulate its internal state.**

This was the conceptual origin of ITNA.

It was not a complete neural model, nor a demonstration of artificial cognition.

It was the first building block.

From that initial principle emerged a much larger question:

> What if an entire artificial system could be constructed from interconnected components capable of receiving signals, maintaining internal states, integrating information, and regulating one another?

## 2. The Vision

NeuroCore aims to investigate whether selected functional principles of biological neural systems can be reconstructed within an artificial architecture.

The objective goes beyond signal analysis, anomaly detection, or behavioral imitation.

Rather than simply programming a machine to reproduce predefined biological responses, the research investigates the underlying functional conditions that make those responses possible.

These include:

- Signal transmission and transformation.
- Internal state continuity and evolution.
- Spatial and temporal information integration.
- Resource availability and recovery.
- State-dependent activation.
- Communication between interconnected units.
- Dynamic stability, adaptation, and collective organization.

The long-term vision is an artificial functional environment inspired by neurobiology.

**The objective is not to imitate the appearance of intelligence, but to investigate the organization of the processes from which complex biological behavior emerges.**

No specific implementation technology is assumed.

Software, electronics, neuromorphic devices, memristive systems, and biohybrid technologies may contribute to different functional processes.

The challenge is to identify which biological properties must be preserved, how they can be represented mathematically, and whether artificial implementations can reproduce them.

Biological equivalence remains an open research question.

## 3. Seven Neurobiological Reference Phases

The proposed architecture is organized into seven functional research areas.

Each phase identifies a biological process and a possible corresponding function within an artificial robotic system.

### Proposed Biological-to-Robotic Mapping

| Phase | Neurobiological Process | Proposed Robotic Function |
|---|---|---|
| **01** | Synaptic Transmission | Acquisition of signals from sensors |
| **02** | Postsynaptic Potentials (EPSP/IPSP) | Transformation of signals into excitatory or inhibitory contributions |
| **03** | Dendritic Integration | Spatial and temporal fusion of information |
| **04** | Subthreshold Dynamics | Continuous updating of internal states |
| **05** | Spike Generation | Event activation when defined conditions are satisfied |
| **06** | Network Propagation | Transmission and coordination between computational modules |
| **07** | Attractor Basins | Organization of operational states, stability, and transitions |

These correspondences are **proposed functional abstractions**, not demonstrated biological equivalents.

Each phase requires:

- Biological reference analysis.
- Mathematical and functional specification.
- Identification of candidate artificial implementations.
- Quantitative verification against relevant reference models.

The phases are not necessarily independent or sequential. Their interactions, feedback mechanisms, and functional dependencies remain subjects of investigation.

**The objective is not to construct seven isolated modules, but to investigate whether their integration can produce a coherent artificial functional architecture.**

## 4. Mathematical Foundation

NeuroCore's mathematical framework provides an initial structure for representing system configurations, state evolution, and dynamic organization.

### Layer 0 — Configuration Space

The initial mathematical foundation introduces a general configuration space:

$$
x(t) \in X
$$

Where:

- $X$ represents the space of admissible system configurations.
- $x(t)$ represents the system state at time $t$.

### Functional Group Specification

Each candidate functional group may be represented through an implementation-independent contract:

$$
G_i =
\left(
X_i,\,
U_i,\,
Y_i,\,
\Theta_i,\,
\mathcal{C}_i,\,
\mathcal{V}_i
\right)
$$

Where:

| Symbol | Definition |
|---|---|
| $X_i$ | Admissible state space |
| $U_i$ | Input space and semantics |
| $Y_i$ | Output space and semantics |
| $\Theta_i$ | Configurable parameters |
| $\mathcal{C}_i$ | Physical and mathematical constraints |
| $\mathcal{V}_i$ | Verification criteria |

This proposed specification allows different technological implementations to be investigated while preserving explicitly defined functional requirements.

### Dynamic Operators

Previous NeuroCore research introduced mathematical operators including:

$$
\Phi,\quad \Delta,\quad \Omega
$$

These operators belong to the broader investigation of temporal variation, dynamic coherence, stability, and regime organization.

Their mathematical definitions, experimental results, and limitations are documented separately.

The correspondence between these operators and the proposed neurobiological architecture has not yet been established.

## 5. From Biology to Artificial Systems

NeuroCore investigates three distinct levels of correspondence between biological processes and artificial implementations.

**Functional Equivalence**

Reproduction of selected operational properties of a biological process.

**Dynamic Equivalence**

Reproduction of relevant temporal relationships, state dependencies, and adaptive behavior.

**Physical Equivalence**

Reproduction of comparable material or biophysical mechanisms.

These levels are not interchangeable.

A software model may reproduce selected functional properties without reproducing the underlying biological mechanisms.

Likewise, a physical device may share certain biological characteristics without demonstrating equivalent system-level behavior.

Each proposed correspondence requires explicit definitions, measurable criteria, and experimental verification.

## 6. NeuroCore and Robotics

Modern robotic systems already employ artificial intelligence for perception, learning, planning, and decision-making.

NeuroCore investigates a complementary architectural foundation:

**An internal functional infrastructure dedicated to state continuity, integration, and regulation.**

The long-term objective is to explore whether artificial systems can maintain and coordinate internal dynamic processes inspired by biological neural organization.

Such an infrastructure could potentially operate alongside conventional control systems and artificial intelligence.

Rather than replacing AI, NeuroCore aims to investigate a functional substrate with which AI systems could interact.

The research does not claim to create subjective consciousness, artificial general intelligence, or a complete biological brain.

Its focus is on observable, measurable, and experimentally testable functional properties.

## 7. Research Development

**Current status: Conceptual architecture under development.**

The proposed seven-phase architecture has not yet been implemented or independently validated as an integrated artificial neural system.

The development programme includes:

1. Biological reference analysis.
2. Mathematical and functional specification.
3. Computational modeling and simulation.
4. Investigation of artificial component alternatives.
5. Quantitative testing against relevant baselines.
6. Hardware–software integration.
7. Independent experimental validation.

Its feasibility, scalability, and biological correspondence remain open questions.

Previous NeuroCore mathematical models and experimental investigations constitute separate research contributions and must not be interpreted as validation of the proposed integrated architecture.

## 8. From the First Building Block to the Architecture

The conceptual evolution of NeuroCore can be summarized as:

$$
\text{Signal}
\rightarrow
\text{Circuit }(r)
\rightarrow
\text{State Dynamics}
\rightarrow
\text{Functional Organization}
\rightarrow
\text{Neuro-Inspired Architecture}
$$

This sequence describes the evolution of the research concept, not an implemented computational pipeline.

ITNA began with the interaction between a sound and a receiving circuit.

Subsequent research expanded toward mathematical operators, dynamic stability, functional continuity, and the organization of internal states.

The current ambition is significantly broader:

**To move from investigating the response of a single abstract circuit to designing an interconnected artificial architecture inspired by the functional organization of the nervous system.**

ITNA did not contain the complete architecture.

It established the initial principle from which the broader research vision emerged.

---

## 9. Long-Term Research Commitment

**NeuroCore is a multi-year research programme, not a project expected to be completed within weeks or months.**

The proposed architecture involves multiple scientific and engineering challenges, including neurobiological modeling, mathematical formalization, computational implementation, physical component development, system integration, and independent experimental validation.

The development of individual functional components may require extensive investigation before integration can be considered.

The complete architecture may require years of interdisciplinary research, experimentation, revision, and collaboration.

No fixed completion date or guarantee of technical feasibility is claimed.

**The priority is scientific consistency, reproducibility, and progressive validation — not rapid implementation.**

---

## Research Statement

NeuroCore is an independent research programme investigating whether selected principles of biological neural organization can be translated into modular artificial systems.

Its mathematical foundation provides a formal starting point, while the seven neurobiological phases define a proposed functional research architecture.

The project does not claim to have reproduced a biological nervous system.

Its objective is to develop a rigorous, progressively testable framework through which artificial functional and dynamic correspondence can be investigated.

**ITNA was not the architecture itself. It was the first building block of a much larger vision.**
